In this study, we have investigated the cross-linguistic sound symbolism in ideophones by employing computational methods on publicly available datasets. The analysis results provide insights into the patterns of sound symbolism shared across languages, focusing on Japanese and English ideophones.

The statistical tests revealed that the predictive accuracies of the models for Japanese and English ideophones were 0.292 and 0.330, respectively. Although the accuracy values indicate a modest ability of the models to predict sound-symbolic patterns, they underscore the complexity and variability inherent in cross-linguistic ideophones, reflecting the challenges in modeling such phenomena accurately. The absence of reported p-values and effect sizes in this analysis suggests that further statistical tests might be necessary to evaluate the significance of these findings robustly. Additionally, confidence intervals were not provided, which could have offered more insight into the precision and reliability of the model estimates.

Figure 1 illustrates the distribution of iconicity scores for Japanese ideophones, providing a visual representation of the range and frequency of iconicity values across the dataset. The histogram shows a concentration around higher iconicity scores, suggesting that Japanese ideophones are perceived as strongly iconic. This aligns with the descriptive statistics, where the mean iconicity score was 5.34 with a standard deviation of 1.55, out of a maximum score of 7. These statistics highlight the marked presence of sound-symbolic characteristics within the Japanese ideophone dataset.

Figure 2 displays the distribution of responses to English ideophones. The plot indicates that the majority of the responses had a mean value of 3.55 with a standard deviation of 1.42, on a scale of 1 to 5. This suggests a moderate level of perceived sound symbolism in English ideophones. The data visualization shows a more uniform distribution compared to Japanese ideophones, reflecting a different pattern of sound symbolism perception in English, which may be indicative of cultural or linguistic differences in the interpretation of ideophonic sounds.

The summary statistics provide further insights into the linguistic features of ideophones across the two languages. For Japanese ideophones, features such as initial obstruency and vowel assonance were analyzed, with initial obstruency showing a high mean of 0.97, indicating a strong presence in Japanese ideophones. The frequency of ideophones had a mean of 569.35, with a wide range evidenced by a standard deviation of 1221.35, highlighting the variability in usage frequency. In contrast, English ideophones were characterized by participant responses and categorical classifications, with the latter showing a balanced mean of 0.5, signifying equivalent distribution across categories.

The key finding from this analysis is the apparent difference in the strength and pattern of sound symbolism between Japanese and English ideophones, as reflected in both statistical and visual data. Although the predictive models showed limited accuracy, the exploration of iconicity scores and response distributions contributes to the understanding of cross-linguistic sound symbolism. These findings have significant implications for linguistic theory, suggesting that while there may be universal tendencies in sound symbolism, cultural and linguistic contexts heavily influence its manifestation. Further research with enhanced models and additional datasets could deepen these insights and aid in developing more effective computational approaches to studying ideophones.
