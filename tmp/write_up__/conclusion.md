In conclusion, this study has systematically explored the sound-symbolic patterns in ideophones within Japanese and English, providing critical insights into the phonosemantic relationships that challenge the traditional linguistic notion of arbitrariness. Our computational models, although achieving modest predictability with accuracies of 29.2% for Japanese and 33.0% for English ideophones, underscore the inherent complexity and variability of sound symbolism across languages. The pronounced sound-symbolic associations in Japanese, as indicated by higher mean iconicity scores, suggest a culturally embedded phonosemantic connection, while English ideophones exhibit greater interpretative diversity.

These findings reinforce the significance of sound symbolism as a pervasive linguistic phenomenon, highlighting its potential as a universal feature tempered by cultural and linguistic nuances. The study contributes to the foundational understanding of cross-linguistic sound symbolism, suggesting that phonetic elements can indeed convey semantic content in a non-arbitrary manner. This research lays the groundwork for future investigations into the intricate dynamics between language, culture, and cognition, advocating for the refinement of computational models and the inclusion of a wider array of linguistic features.

Ultimately, this study underscores the importance of interdisciplinary approaches in uncovering the complexities of sound symbolism. By bridging computational linguistics and cultural analysis, we can further elucidate the role of sound in meaning-making across languages. Future research should continue to explore these dimensions, expanding the scope of phonosemantic studies and enhancing our grasp of the universal and variable aspects of human language. Through such endeavors, we can better appreciate the rich tapestry of linguistic expression, where sound and meaning dynamically converge.
