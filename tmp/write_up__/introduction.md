Sound symbolism, the non-arbitrary link between phonetic sounds and meaning, has long fascinated linguists due to its potential to unveil universal principles underpinning human language. Historically, linguistic theory posited that the relationship between sounds and meanings was largely arbitrary, as famously articulated by Saussure. However, the phenomenon of sound symbolism challenges this view by demonstrating consistent patterns where specific phonetic features systematically correlate with particular semantic domains. This study seeks to investigate such patterns in ideophones across multiple languages, thereby contributing to a burgeoning area of research that intersects phonology, semantics, and computational linguistics.

The study of sound symbolism has gained considerable momentum over the past few decades, particularly with advancements in computational methodologies and the increasing availability of linguistic datasets. Research by Alper and Averbuch-Elor (2023) has demonstrated that vision-and-language models can reflect sound-symbolic mappings, such as the well-documented kiki-bouba effect, thereby offering new avenues for exploring sound symbolism using artificial intelligence (AI) tools. The study by Kilpatrick et al. (2023), which employs random forests to explore sound symbolism in the context of Pokémon names, underscores the potential of machine learning algorithms to model and predict sound-meaning associations in a systematic manner. These studies illustrate the promise of computational approaches in uncovering the underlying principles of sound symbolism.

Despite these advancements, significant gaps remain in our understanding, particularly in the realm of cross-linguistic sound symbolism. While individual languages have been studied in detail, there exists a paucity of research that systematically examines ideophones across diverse linguistic contexts. Ideophones, characterized by their vivid sensory depictions, provide a fertile ground for examining sound symbolism due to their inherently expressive nature. This research aims to fill this gap by leveraging existing databases of ideophones from languages such as Japanese, Russian, Haitian Creole, and English. These datasets, publicly available and well-documented, offer a robust foundation for a cross-linguistic analysis of sound-symbolic patterns.

The computational exploration of sound symbolism also intersects with broader developments in AI, particularly in the realm of model transparency and interpretability. As highlighted by Adadi and Berrada (2018) in their discussion of explainable AI, the black-box nature of many machine learning models poses challenges to understanding the mechanisms driving their predictions. Applying explainable AI techniques in the study of sound symbolism could provide insights into the cognitive processes that underlie sound-meaning associations, thereby enhancing both our theoretical understanding and practical applications of these findings.

Furthermore, the exploration of sound symbolism in ideophones aligns with contemporary linguistic theories that emphasize the interplay between arbitrariness and systematicity in language. Akinbo's (2023) study on reduplication and repetition in Fungwa illustrates the non-arbitrary aspects of sound-meaning mappings, which coexist with the arbitrary elements of language. This duality is central to understanding how languages encode meaning and how speakers interpret these encodings across different contexts.

In summary, this research addresses an essential gap in the field by examining cross-linguistic sound symbolism in ideophones through computational methods. By harnessing the power of publicly available datasets and advanced machine learning algorithms, this study aims to uncover universal patterns of sound symbolism that transcend individual languages. The outcomes are expected to have significant implications for our understanding of linguistic universals, the cognitive basis of language, and the application of AI in linguistic research. This work stands at the intersection of phonology, semantics, and computational linguistics, promising to advance our theoretical and practical grasp of sound symbolism in human language.
